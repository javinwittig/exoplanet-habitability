Before We Begin

What is an exoplanet?

An exoplanet — short for extrasolar planet — is any planet that orbits a star other than our Sun. Our solar system has eight planets, but the Milky Way holds hundreds of billions of stars, and many of them have their own planets. Since the first confirmed detection in 1992, astronomers have found over 5,700 exoplanets, with thousands more awaiting confirmation.

How do we find them?

We can't photograph most exoplanets directly — they're too faint next to their host stars. Instead, we detect them indirectly: the transit method watches for tiny dips in starlight as a planet crosses in front of its star, and radial velocity measures the wobble a planet's gravity induces in its star. Both methods yield measurements like orbital period, star temperature, and planet radius — the raw features our model trains on.

Why does it matter?

Finding habitable exoplanets is the first step toward answering whether we're alone in the universe. A planet in the Habitable Zone — where temperatures allow liquid water — is the most promising candidate for hosting life. But with thousands of candidates, astronomers need machine learning to sift through the data and flag the most promising worlds for further study.

Step 01 — The Catalog

1 715 known exoplanets.

Every dot on this screen is a real, confirmed world discovered beyond our solar system — sourced directly from the NASA Exoplanet Archive via its TAP API. The data is queried as a SQL statement against the pscomppars table, pulling nine measurements for each planet: orbital period, mass, insolation, radius, and five stellar properties.

The raw query

SELECT pl_orbper, pl_bmasse, pl_insol, pl_rade, st_teff, st_logg, st_rad, st_mass, sy_vmag FROM pscomppars — this fetches every confirmed planet with complete measurements. After dropping rows with missing values in the key columns (insolation, radius), we're left with 1,715 worlds.

Defining "habitable"

A planet is labeled habitable if its insolation falls between 0.25–1.5 Earth units and its radius between 0.5–1.6 Earth radii. This captures the classic Habitable Zone: warm enough for liquid water, small enough to be rocky. Only 0.6% of all planets meet these criteria — an extreme class imbalance the model has to deal with.

Step 02 — The Prediction

A Random Forest searches for candidates.

Here's the twist: the model never sees insolation or radius directly. Instead, it only uses seven stellar and orbital features — orbital period, planet mass, star temperature, surface gravity, star radius, star mass, and visual magnitude — to predict whether a planet falls in the habitable zone.

Why hide the obvious features?

This is a realistic scenario. When astronomers discover a new exoplanet, they often don't yet have precise radius or insolation measurements. But they do have data about the host star and the planet's orbit. The model learns to infer habitability from these indirect signals — mimicking how real exoplanet surveys work.

Step 03 — The Confirmation

Which predictions actually hold up?

Now we cross-reference the model's predictions against the actual habitability criteria (insolation between 0.25–1.5 and radius between 0.5–1.6 Earth radii). The teal dots are true hits — planets the model correctly identified. The faded dots were false alarms: the model was cautious, flagging too many rather than risking to miss a potentially habitable world.

The precision-recall tradeoff

We chose a threshold of 0.17 (instead of the default 0.5) to maximize recall — catching 80% of truly habitable planets at the cost of more false positives. In astrobiology, missing a habitable world is worse than investigating a false lead.

Step 05 — How It Works

The Pipeline

The model is a scikit-learn Pipeline with three stages, designed to handle real-world data with missing values.

1

SimpleImputer

Replaces missing values with the median of each feature. Real astronomical data is full of gaps — not every measurement is available for every star.

2

StandardScaler

Normalizes all features to zero mean and unit variance. Star temperatures (thousands of Kelvin) and orbital periods (days to years) live on completely different scales — scaling puts them on equal footing.

3

RandomForestClassifier

An ensemble of 100 decision trees, each trained on a random subset of the data. With class_weight="balanced", the model upweights the rare habitable class (only 0.6% of all planets) to prevent it from being drowned out by the overwhelming majority of non-habitable worlds.

Why a custom threshold?

The default classification threshold of 0.5 is often suboptimal for imbalanced datasets. We used precision-recall curves on a validation set to find the threshold (0.17) that achieves at least 90% recall — ensuring we catch most habitable candidates while keeping false positives manageable.
